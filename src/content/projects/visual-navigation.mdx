<p class="vn-lede">MiniGrid&#8217;s LavaCrossing puts an agent in a 9&#215;9 room cut by rivers of lava. Each river has exactly one gap. The agent can turn left, turn right or step forward, and it has to reach the green square without touching lava.</p>

The families differ in how many rivers there are: S9N1 has one, S9N2 two, S9N3 three. Within a family, where the rivers run and where the gaps sit changes every episode.

An agent that learned to *look for the gap* should carry over to a new room. One that learned a route won&#8217;t. I put five approaches through that test.

<p class="vn-fno">finding 1</p>

### Memorizing looks like learning until the map moves

Single-env PPO solved 40 of the 42 one-river layouts, 95.2%. By the usual measure it was done. Add a second river and it succeeded 20% of the time. A third, 10%. Its wins took 13 to 17 steps, on rooms that happened to look like home.

The same algorithm trained across all three families held at <mark>100%, 98.4% and 97.8%</mark> after 40M steps. Same network, same reward. What changed was what it saw. The failure was about training coverage, not reinforcement learning as such.

<p class="vn-fno">finding 2</p>

### Thinking out loud made the language model worse

Zero-shot, GPT-4o-mini solved 20 of 20 sampled episodes in every family, the same as the planner. Asked to reason step by step in a structured format first, it fell to 85%.

The format makes it restate where the gap is at every step, and it doesn&#8217;t always say the same thing: gap on the left, then gap on the right, on a map that hasn&#8217;t changed. Then it acts on whichever it said last. Gap-position inconsistency went from 4.1% of reasoning steps to 24.3%.

It wasn&#8217;t simply worse at reasoning. Direction confusion fell from 29.4% to 10.6%, and the share of coherent steps barely moved. It traded one mistake for a more fatal one.

These rates come from an LLM judge (GPT-4o-mini grading the traces) that hasn&#8217;t been validated against human labels yet. Read them as a likely mechanism, not a proof.

<p class="vn-fno">finding 3</p>

### Right steps, failed trips

Behavior cloning copied a perfect planner, and DAgger added three rounds of corrections on the states the agent actually reached. It picked the planner&#8217;s next move 85.2% of the time and finished 18% of its one-river trips.

That&#8217;s compounding error. One wrong turn puts the agent somewhere the demonstrations never went, and the next step is more likely to be wrong too. Fourteen steps at 85% each leaves about one trip in ten clean. Drag the slider to see how fast it falls.

### Eight times the frames per second

40M steps is a lot of waiting at 200 frames per second. I profiled before scaling up and found three causes:

<ul class="vn-ticks">
<li>The reward wrapper rendered every observation a second time. It now reads the agent&#8217;s position directly.</li>
<li>One environment left the GPU idle while the CPU stepped it. Now 8 run in parallel.</li>
<li>A CNN on a 7&#215;7 input cost more in GPU overhead than it saved, so that run moved to an MLP on the CPU.</li>
</ul>

Same hyperparameters, about 1,600 frames per second.

<div class="vn-prose">

### Reading the reasoning, at scale

Success rates say whether the language model got there, not how. So I built an LLM-as-judge pipeline: one call per episode, every reasoning step sorted into one of five categories. It classified <mark>1,671 steps across 40 episodes</mark>, 591 answering directly and 1,080 with chain-of-thought. The CoT finding comes from there.

The judge is the same model as the agent, which is why that finding carries a caveat. A small hand-labeled check is the obvious next step.

</div>
<Card k="The judge&#8217;s five categories">
<ul class="vn-cats">
<li><code>gap_position_inconsistency</code><span>Puts the gap somewhere the step before didn&#8217;t, on the same map.</span></li>
<li><code>direction_confusion</code><span>Misreads which way it&#8217;s facing.</span></li>
<li><code>goal_hallucination</code><span>Mentions a goal or feature the rest of the trace contradicts.</span></li>
<li><code>spatial_commitment_failure</code><span>States a plan, then acts against it in the same step.</span></li>
<li><code>correct</code><span>Coherent with the steps around it.</span></li>
</ul>

<FolderSheet id="limits" title="What it doesn&#8217;t show" tab="Caveats">
<Row>
<div class="vn-prose">

<ul class="vn-caveats">
<li>A small, static grid you can see all of, with a goal you can say in one sentence. That favors the language model. A partial view, moving parts or long horizons could change the ranking.</li>
<li>Every RL number is one training seed.</li>
<li>Only single-env PPO was tested on rooms it never saw. Randomized PPO trained on all three families.</li>
<li>The language model ran 20 episodes per family. 20 of 20 means somewhere between 83.9% and 100%.</li>
<li>Randomized PPO&#8217;s three-river score covers 276 of the 313 layouts found; the rarest couldn&#8217;t be reproduced in 20,000 tries.</li>
<li>The chain-of-thought mechanism rests on a judge that hasn&#8217;t been checked against people.</li>
</ul>

These are findings about this setting, not a verdict on reinforcement learning against language models. It&#8217;s also one project: the first thing I built at PAIR Lab, not the whole of my work there. More of it will follow.
