<Section n="§1" title="The question">
<Row note="<sup>1</sup> S9 is the grid size; N is the number of lava walls. Domain-randomized PPO was evaluated on 42, 249 and 276 distinct layout patterns in the three families.">

<p class="lede">MiniGrid&#8217;s LavaCrossing puts an agent in a 9&#8202;&#215;&#8202;9 room cut by walls of lava. Each wall has exactly one gap. The agent has to reach the goal square without stepping in lava.</p>

The variants differ in how many walls there are: one in S9N1, two in S9N2, three in S9N3.<Mark n="1" /> Within a family, the walls and gaps can sit in many places.

A policy that only ever sees one layout can score well on it without learning anything about gaps. I wanted to know how badly that breaks when the layout changes, what fixes it, and whether a vision-language model that was never trained on the task does any better.

</Row>
</Section>

<Section n="§2" title="Five approaches">
<Row wide>
<Table caption="Table 1. What each approach learns from">

| Approach | How it gets its behavior |
| --- | --- |
| Single-environment PPO | Reinforcement learning on one layout family (S9N1). |
| Domain-randomized PPO | Reinforcement learning across all three families, randomized per episode, 40M steps. |
| BC + DAgger | Imitates 1,500 BFS demonstrations, then three rounds of the expert relabeling the states the agent actually reaches. |
| Zero-shot GPT-4o-mini | No task training at all. Looks at a rendered image of the grid and chooses moves. |
| BFS planner | Not learned. Searches the full grid state directly: the ceiling for what is possible. |

</Table>
</Row>
</Section>

<Finding title="Memorizing is not navigating" value="10%" caption="Single-environment PPO on three walls.">

Single-environment PPO reached 95.2% on the layout family it trained in, then fell to 20% with two walls and 10% with three. It had learned a route, not the idea of a gap. Fig. 1 replays that failure: the dotted agent repeats its training route wherever the gap happens to be.

</Finding>

<Finding title="Variety fixes it" value="97.8%" caption="Domain-randomized PPO on three walls, over 276 layout patterns.">

Train the same algorithm across many randomized layouts and the collapse mostly disappears: 100%, 98.4% and 97.8% across the three families, after 40M steps. The failure was about training coverage, not reinforcement learning as such.

</Finding>

<Finding title="Thinking out loud made the model worse" value="85%" caption="Success with chain-of-thought prompting, down from 100% without it.">

Zero-shot, GPT-4o-mini with a full rendered view solved every sampled episode. Asking it to reason step by step dropped success from 100% to 85%, and its account of where the gap was became much less consistent: gap-position inconsistency went from 4.1% to 24.3% of reasoning steps. That last number comes from an LLM judge that hasn&#8217;t been validated against human labels yet.

</Finding>

<Finding title="Good steps, bad trips" value="18%" caption="Rollout success for BC + DAgger, despite 85.2% step accuracy.">

Behavior cloning with DAgger picked the expert&#8217;s move 85.2% of the time, yet finished only 18% of rollouts. Small mistakes put the agent in states the expert never showed it, and the errors compound.

</Finding>
</Section>

<Section n="§4" title="Making it run, and reading the reasoning">
<Finding value="8&#215;" caption="Training throughput, about 200 to 1,600 frames per second.">

Training was the bottleneck. Removing redundant observation generation, running eight environments in parallel and cutting GPU overhead the job didn&#8217;t need took throughput from about 200 to about 1,600 frames per second.

</Finding>

<Finding value="1,671" caption="VLM reasoning steps classified, across 40 episodes.">

Success rates say whether the language model got there, not how. So I built an LLM-as-judge pipeline with a five-category spatial-reasoning taxonomy and used it to classify the 1,671 reasoning steps the model wrote across 40 navigation episodes.

</Finding>
</Section>

<Section n="§5" title="What it doesn't show">
<Row>

This is a small, static, fully observable grid world with a verbally simple goal, which favors the language model. These are findings about this setting, not a verdict on reinforcement learning versus language models. The full write-up, methods and caveats included, is the 17-page paper in the repository.

</Row>
</Section>
